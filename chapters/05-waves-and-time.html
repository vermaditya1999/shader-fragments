Waves and time: sin, cos and animation

A shader forgets everything between frames, yet shaders are full of motion. The trick is a clock that every pixel can read, and a function that turns a clock into smooth back-and-forth movement. That function is the sine, and this chapter builds it from a point walking around a circle.

5.1Angles, and why radians

You probably measure angles in degrees: a full turn is 360°, a right angle is 90°. That number 360 is an old human choice. Mathematics, and every GLSL function, uses a more natural unit, the radian.

Math from zero: the radian

Take a circle of radius 1. Start at the right-hand edge and walk counterclockwise along the circle. When you have walked a distance of exactly 1 (one radius), the angle you've turned through is one radian, about 57.3°.

The whole way round a circle of radius r is 2πr long, where π ≈ 3.14159. For radius 1 that's 2π ≈ 6.28318. So:

full turn = 2π radians ≈ 6.28318 (360°) half turn = π radians ≈ 3.14159 (180°) quarter = π/2 ≈ 1.5708 (90°)

The nice thing about radians: the angle and the distance walked along a unit circle are the same number. To convert, radians(degrees) and degrees(radians) are built in, or multiply degrees by π / 180. You'll see the constant 6.28318 (2π, sometimes called τ, “tau”) all over shader code. It means “one full turn”.

5.2The unit circle: where sin and cos come from

Put a circle of radius 1 around the origin, called the unit circle. Pick an angle θ (the Greek letter theta) measured counterclockwise from the positive x axis, and walk to that point on the circle. That point has an x coordinate and a y coordinate, and those two numbers have names:

cos θ = the x of the point at angle θ sin θ = the y of the point at angle θ

That's the actual definition: cosine and sine are the coordinates of a point on the unit circle. Drag the point below, or press Play and watch what its x and y do as it goes round:

A few facts fall straight out of the picture:

  • Both always stay between -1 and 1, because the circle has radius 1.
  • They repeat every full turn, 2π. A function that repeats is called periodic, and 2π is its period.
  • At θ = 0 the point is at (1, 0): cos 0 = 1 and sin 0 = 0. A quarter turn later, at the top, it's (0, 1).
  • Cosine is sine shifted by a quarter turn. They're the same wave, started at different times.
  • By Pythagoras (Chapter 7), x² + y² = 1 on the unit circle, so sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 always.

5.3The sine wave

Plot sin θ against θ and you get the most important curve in graphics: the smooth hump that rises, falls and rises again forever. This plotter is set up for waves: the grid lines on the x axis are quarter turns (π/2 apart) and the bright horizontal lines are at -1, 0 and 1.

5.4Amplitude, frequency and phase

Three numbers reshape a sine wave, and you'll use all three constantly:

y = amplitude * sin(frequency * x + phase)
  • Amplitude stretches it vertically. 0.5 × sin(x) swings between -0.5 and 0.5.
  • Frequency squeezes it horizontally. sin(2x) completes two cycles in the time sin(x) does one, so its period is 2π / 2 = π.
  • Phase slides it sideways. sin(x + π/2) is the cosine.

Load it into the workbench, click on 0.0 after phase and drag the slider: the wave slides sideways. Keep that in mind, because in a moment the phase is going to be time.

5.5From -1..1 to 0..1

Colors live between 0 and 1, but sine swings between -1 and 1. You already know the fix from Chapter 3: multiply by 0.5 (now -0.5..0.5) and add 0.5 (now 0..1).

0.5 + 0.5 * sin(x) → swings smoothly between 0 and 1

You'll see this exact expression in a huge number of shaders.

5.6Time: iTime

Now the clock. The runner provides iTime, a float holding the number of seconds since the shader started: 0.0, then 0.016, 0.033… growing by about 1/60 every frame. It's a uniform, so every pixel sees the same value in a given frame.

A number that just grows isn't motion yet. Feed it to sine and it becomes a smooth swing:

Multiplying the time changes the speed: sin(iTime * 2.0) cycles twice as fast. A cycle every second needs a frequency of 2π: sin(iTime * 6.28318).

Notice what is not happening. No pixel remembers anything. Each frame, each pixel computes its color fresh from the current time. The motion exists only because the input (iTime) changes and the output follows.

5.7Waves that travel

Now combine space and time in one sine. Each pixel uses its own x as part of the phase:

Read it as amplitude, frequency and phase: the frequency in space is 20 (that's 20 / 2π ≈ 3.2 stripes across the canvas), and the phase is -iTime * 3.0, which decreases steadily. You saw what changing the phase does: it slides the wave. A steadily changing phase slides it steadily, so the stripes travel to the right.

Change the minus to a plus: sin(uv.x * 20.0 + iTime * 3.0). Which way do the stripes move now? What about 20.0 → 60.0?

With +, the stripes travel to the left. Here's one way to see it: a stripe is where the inside of the sine has a fixed value, say 0. With +, that's where uv.x × 20 = −iTime × 3, so as time grows the stripe's x must shrink: it moves left. With 60 instead of 20, the stripes are three times narrower and, at the same phase speed, move three times slower across the screen.

// AHA

Animation is a function of time. Ask “what color is this pixel at time t?”, and motion appears by itself, without anything being moved or remembered.

5.8Adding waves together

Add two waves and you get a new, more complicated wave. Where both are high, the sum is higher; where one is high and the other low, they cancel. This is interference, and with waves running in different directions it makes the classic “plasma” effect:

The last line uses a trick worth understanding now, because Chapter 6 builds on it. v * 3.0 + vec3(0.0, 2.0, 4.0) is a vec3: the same number with three different offsets. cos is applied slot by slot, so red, green and blue each get a cosine of v, shifted by a different phase. As v changes, the three channels rise and fall out of step, and that cycles through hues.

Adding iTime and three phases 0, 2, 4 gives each channel its own cosine (above). 0.5 + 0.5 * … remaps -1..1 to 0..1 (this chapter). Notice vec3(0, 2, 4) uses ints inside a constructor, which GLSL allows.

5.9Other repeating shapes

Sine is the smoothest repeating shape, but not the only one. Using the tools from Chapter 4:

wavecode (0..1, one cycle per unit)character
sine0.5 + 0.5 * sin(x * 6.28318)smooth, natural
sawtoothfract(x)rises, then snaps back
triangleabs(fract(x) - 0.5) * 2.0linear up and down
squarestep(0.5, fract(x))on, off, on, off

What you now know

  • Angles in GLSL are in radians: a full turn is 2π ≈ 6.28318.
  • cos θ and sin θ are the x and y of the point at angle θ on the unit circle; both swing between -1 and 1 and repeat every 2π.
  • amplitude * sin(frequency * x + phase): stretch, squeeze, slide.
  • 0.5 + 0.5 * sin(x) remaps a wave to 0..1.
  • iTime is seconds since start. Putting it in the phase of a sine makes motion; mixing it with position makes waves travel.
  • Adding waves makes interference; a vec3 of phase offsets gives each color channel its own wave.
Exercises

1. Make the canvas fade between black and white once every 2 seconds.

Hint

One cycle every 2 seconds means the inside of sin should grow by 2π every 2 seconds: iTime * 3.14159.

2. Make vertical stripes that move upward.

Solution
vec2 uv = fragCoord / iResolution.xy;
float v = 0.5 + 0.5 * sin(uv.y * 30.0 - iTime * 4.0);   // stripes along y travel up
fragColor = vec4(vec3(v), 1.0);

3. In the interference example, add a fourth wave that depends on the distance from the center, length(uv). What shape do its crests make?

Answer

v += sin(length(uv) * 10.0 - t * 2.0); (and divide by 4.0). Its crests are rings, because all points at the same distance from the center are a circle. The rings travel outward.
