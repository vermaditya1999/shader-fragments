Vectors: arrows, distances and directions

You've used vec2 as "two numbers in a box" since Chapter 2. This chapter gives those two numbers a picture: an arrow. Once you see vectors as arrows, distance, direction and lighting all turn out to be a few lines of arithmetic, and by the end you'll shade a 3D-looking ball with a single dot product.

7.1A point or an arrow

The same two numbers can mean two different things, and shader code switches between them constantly:

  • A position: "the place at x = 2, y = 1". This is what uv is: where the current pixel is.
  • A step or arrow: "go 2 to the right and 1 up", from wherever you are. An arrow has a direction (which way it points) and a length (how far it goes), but no fixed starting place.

The word vector covers both. A position is really an arrow from the origin to that place, so you can always switch views. In the diagrams below, vectors are drawn as arrows from the origin, and you can drag their tips.

7.2Adding and subtracting

Adding two vectors adds their parts: x to x, y to y. As arrows, it means walk the first arrow, then the second, and the sum is the arrow from where you started to where you ended up. Drag the tips of a and b:

Now switch the widget to b − a. Subtracting gives the arrow from a's tip to b's tip. Read it as "where do I have to go, starting at a, to get to b?" Check it: start at a and add b − a, and you get a + b − a = b.

This is the most useful idea in the chapter. In shaders it shows up as uv - center: the arrow from some center point to the current pixel. It measures every pixel's position as seen from the center, as if the center were the origin. Here the center wanders around, and a grid drawn in the measured-from-center coordinates comes along with it:

Nothing here moves the grid directly. Each pixel just measures itself from c, and the grid appears wherever those measurements land on a multiple of 0.25. Chapter 9 builds all of moving, rotating and scaling on this idea.

7.3Scaling

Multiplying a vector by a single number (a scalar) multiplies both parts. As an arrow, it keeps the direction and stretches the length: v * 2.0 is twice as long, v * 0.5 half, and v * -1.0 (or just -v) the same length pointing the opposite way. Zooming with uv * 3.0 in Chapter 3 was exactly this, applied to every pixel's position at once.

7.4Length: Pythagoras from zero

How long is the arrow (3, 2)? It goes 3 across and 2 up, and those two parts make the sides of a right-angled triangle, with the arrow itself as the long side. The length of that long side comes from the most useful theorem in graphics.

Math from zero: Pythagoras' theorem

For a right-angled triangle with short sides x and y and long side c: c² = x² + y². Here's why, using the right-hand picture.

The big square has side x + y, so its area is (x + y)². The same square is also made of four triangles plus the tilted square in the middle. Each triangle is half of an x-by-y rectangle, so its area is ½xy, and the tilted square's area is c². So:

(x + y)² = 4 × ½xy + c² x² + 2xy + y² = 2xy + c² x² + y² = c²

To get c itself, undo the squaring with a square root: √n is the number that, multiplied by itself, gives n. So √9 = 3, and √2 ≈ 1.414. In GLSL it's sqrt(n).

So the length of the vector (x, y) is √(x² + y²). For (3, 2), that's √13 ≈ 3.61. GLSL has it built in as length(v), and it works the same in 3D: the length of (x, y, z) is √(x² + y² + z²).

The distance between two points is the length of the arrow between them: length(b - a), also built in as distance(a, b). That's the circle from Chapter 3, now fully explained: length(uv) < 0.5 asks "is this pixel within 0.5 of the origin?"

Here's a picture made of nothing but distances. Two points wander; each pixel asks which one is closer and colors itself accordingly, with faint rings marking equal distances:

What shape is the border between the blue and orange regions, and why?

A straight line, always exactly halfway between the two points and at right angles to the line joining them. Every point on it is the same distance from a and from b. With many points instead of two, these borders form a cell pattern called a Voronoi diagram, which you'll build in Chapter 11.

7.5What "distance" means is a choice

A circle is "all the points at the same distance from the center". Pythagoras is the straight-line distance, but it's not the only way to measure. A taxi in a city of square blocks can only drive along the grid, so its distance is |x| + |y|. Or you could measure by whichever part is bigger: max(|x|, |y|). Change the measure, and the "circles" change shape:

Left: the straight-line distance gives circles. Middle: the taxicab distance gives diamonds. Right: the bigger-part distance gives squares. Every shape in Chapter 8 starts from a question like this: how far is this pixel from the shape?

A shape is just "everything closer than some distance". Change the formula for distance, and the shape changes with it.

7.6Direction without length: normalize

Sometimes you only care which way an arrow points, not how long it is. Divide a vector by its own length and you get an arrow in the same direction with length exactly 1, called a unit vector. This is normalize(v), the same as v / length(v).

Coloring every pixel by the direction from the center (not the distance) gives a picture where everything along the same line from the center gets the same color:

Watch out: normalize(0)

The zero vector (0, 0) has no direction, and normalizing it divides by zero. The result is undefined, usually NaN ("not a number"), which can show up as black or garbage pixels. If a vector could be zero, guard it or add a tiny amount first. Chapter 19 shows how to hunt NaNs down.

7.7The dot product

The dot product of two vectors multiplies their matching parts and adds the results:

dot(a, b) = a.x * b.x + a.y * b.y (in 3D, add a.z * b.z too)

It looks like arbitrary bookkeeping, but it answers a very geometric question: how much do these two arrows point the same way?

Play with it and you'll see the pattern:

  • Pointing the same way: large and positive.
  • At right angles (perpendicular): exactly 0.
  • Pointing opposite ways: negative.
Math from zero: why the dot product measures angle

Turn the whole picture so that a points along the x axis. Turning doesn't change the arrows' lengths or the angle θ between them, and (as you'll see in Chapter 9) it doesn't change the dot product either. Now a = (|a|, 0), where |a| means a's length, and by Chapter 5's unit circle, b = |b| × (cos θ, sin θ). So:

dot(a, b) = |a| × |b| cos θ + 0 × |b| sin θ = |a| × |b| × cos θ

For two unit vectors, the lengths are 1, and the dot product is simply cos θ: 1 when they agree, 0 at a right angle, −1 when opposite. And if only b is a unit vector, dot(a, b) = |a| cos θ, which is how far a reaches along b's direction, like the length of a's shadow on b's line. That's the pink bar in the widget, called the projection of a onto b.

One more gift: dot(v, v) = x² + y², the length squared, with no square root. Shaders use it when they only need to compare distances.

Gradients at any angle

Projection has a beautiful use. If dir is a unit vector, then dot(uv, dir) is how far along dir each pixel is. That's a ramp in any direction you like. In fact uv.x was always just dot(uv, vec2(1.0, 0.0)):

Which side of a line?

A straight line can be described by one point on it, p, and a unit arrow n sticking straight out of it (at right angles), called its normal. For any pixel, dot(uv - p, n) is how far the pixel is along n, measured from the line: positive on one side, negative on the other, zero on the line. Its absolute value is the distance to the line:

The orange side is the side the normal points to. The contour lines run parallel to the line, because every point on one of them is the same distance from it.

A lit ball from one dot product

Here's where the dot product earns its fame. A surface is brightest when it faces the light head-on and gets darker as it turns away, until it's dark when it faces away. "How much does the surface face the light?" is exactly the dot product of two unit vectors: the direction the surface faces (its normal) and the direction toward the light. This is called diffuse or Lambert lighting.

For a ball of radius 1 seen from the front, every pixel inside the circle sees a point on the ball's surface. Pythagoras in 3D tells you how far that point sticks out toward you, z = √(1 − x² − y²). And on a ball, the outward direction at a surface point is just the arrow from the center to that point, so the normal is (x, y, z) itself:

No 3D engine, no mesh, no lights in a scene graph. One square root, one normalize and one dot product per pixel. Every 3D shader in Chapters 13 and 14 lights its surfaces with this same line.

The dot product measures agreement between directions. That one idea gives you gradients at any angle, the side of a line, distance to a line, projection and lighting.

7.8A quarter turn: the perpendicular

In 2D there's a neat trick for turning an arrow a quarter turn counterclockwise: swap its parts and negate the new x. The perpendicular of (x, y) is (−y, x). Check with the dot product: x × (−y) + y × x = 0, so they're at right angles. In GLSL: vec2(-v.y, v.x). Chapter 9 uses it to build rotation, and Chapter 8 uses it for line segments.

7.9Vectors in 3D, and the cross product

Everything above works the same for vec3: add, subtract, scale, length, distance, normalize and dot. 3D adds one new operation, the cross product. cross(a, b) returns a new vector that is perpendicular to both a and b:

vec3 up      = vec3(0.0, 1.0, 0.0);
vec3 forward = vec3(0.0, 0.0, 1.0);
vec3 side    = cross(up, forward);   // (1, 0, 0): at right angles to both

Its length is |a| × |b| × sin θ, so it's zero when a and b point the same way. You won't need it until Chapter 14, where it builds a camera that can look at any point: given the direction the camera faces and which way is up, the cross product finds "right".

7.10Colors are vectors too

A vec3 color is a vector in the RGB cube from Chapter 6, and the same operations have meanings there. mix slides along the straight line between two colors. Luminance, dot(col, vec3(0.299, 0.587, 0.114)), was a dot product all along: how far the color reaches in the direction of "perceived brightness". And distance(colorA, colorB) is a rough measure of how different two colors are.

What you now know

  • A vector is a position or an arrow. Add arrows by walking one then the other; b - a is the arrow from a to b.
  • uv - c measures every pixel from c, as if c were the origin.
  • Pythagoras: length = √(x² + y²). length(v), distance(a, b) = length(b - a).
  • A shape is "everything closer than d"; changing how you measure distance changes the shape.
  • normalize(v) keeps the direction and sets the length to 1. Never normalize zero.
  • dot(a, b) = |a| |b| cos θ: positive when aligned, 0 when perpendicular, negative when opposite. With a unit vector it measures "how far along".
  • Lighting: brightness = max(dot(normal, light), 0.0).
  • Perpendicular in 2D: vec2(-v.y, v.x). In 3D, cross(a, b) is perpendicular to both.

3. Make the ball's light follow the mouse.

Solution
vec2 m = (iMouse.xy * 2.0 - iResolution.xy) / iResolution.y;   // mouse in uv units
vec3 light = normalize(vec3(m, 0.7));                          // up and out of the screen

4. In dot-ramp, what happens if dir isn't a unit vector, say dir * 2.0? Predict it, then try it.

Answer

The dot product doubles everywhere, so the ramp changes twice as fast and the stripes are packed twice as tightly. The direction stays the same. That's why directions are kept at length 1: then the dot product measures real distance.
