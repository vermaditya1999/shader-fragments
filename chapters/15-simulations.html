Living simulations: memory and feedback

Every shader so far has had amnesia: each frame is computed from scratch, from the pixel's position and the time. This chapter gives shaders a memory. Let a shader read what it drew last frame, and it can simulate things that evolve: trails, cellular life, chemical patterns like a leopard's spots, and dye swirling in water, with a million cells updating at once.

15.1Textures: images a shader can read

A texture is an image stored on the GPU that a shader can read from. It's a grid of texels (texture pixels), each holding four numbers, red, green, blue and alpha. Shaders read textures through a sampler, a uniform such as Shadertoy's iChannel0, with two functions:

vec4 c = texture(iChannel0, uv);                 // uv runs 0..1 across the whole texture
vec4 t = texelFetch(iChannel0, ivec2(12, 7), 0); // the exact texel in column 12, row 7
ivec2 size = textureSize(iChannel0, 0);          // how many texels wide and tall it is

texture takes normalized coordinates: (0, 0) is the bottom-left corner of the image and (1, 1) the top-right, whatever its size in texels. When uv lands between texel centers, the GPU can blend the four nearest texels: that's linear filtering, and it's exactly Chapter 11's bilinear blend, done for free by the hardware. With nearest filtering it simply returns the closest texel, so magnified textures look blocky. And when uv goes outside 0..1, the wrap mode decides what happens: repeat the texture (like fract), or clamp to the edge.

texelFetch skips all of that and reads one exact texel by its integer column and row (the last argument is the "mip level", always 0 for us). It's the right tool when texels are data, like cells in a simulation, rather than an image.

On Shadertoy you can plug images, videos and webcams into the iChannels. In this course, the channels carry two things: a buffer the shader drew itself (this chapter), and sound (Chapter 16).

15.2Memory: drawing into a buffer

Normally the output of a fragment shader goes to the screen. But the GPU can also be told to draw into a texture instead, through what's called a framebuffer. Draw into a texture this frame, read it back next frame, and the shader has memory.

One rule complicates it: a pass can't read from the same texture it's writing into, because thousands of pixels run at once and they'd be reading values that other pixels are halfway through overwriting. The standard fix is two textures that take turns, called ping-pong:

The workbench handles all of this. Examples in this chapter have two tabs, following Shadertoy's layout:

  • Buffer A draws into a hidden texture. Its iChannel0 is its own output from the previous frame.
  • Image draws to the screen as usual. Its iChannel0 is Buffer A's newest output.

The simplest thing to do with memory: keep most of last frame and add something new. Here a glowing dot wanders around (drag on the canvas to steer it), and each frame the old picture is kept at 97% brightness, so the past fades slowly, like a long-exposure photo:

Change 0.97 to 0.999. Then to 0.7. What changes, and why?

At 0.999 the trail barely fades: after 60 frames (one second) it still has 0.99960 ≈ 94% of its brightness, so the whole path stays and builds into a bright tangle. At 0.7, after just 10 frames only 0.710 ≈ 3% remains, so the trail is a short comet tail. It's Chapter 14's fog again: the same fraction lost per step is an exponential decay, just counted in frames instead of distance.

The first frame, and why buffers store floats

A buffer starts out empty: all zeros. When a simulation needs a starting state, set it up on the first frame, using the uniform iFrame (the frame counter, starting at 0): if (iFrame == 0) { ...set up...; return; }. In this workbench, resizing the canvas also restarts the buffer.

The buffer stores 32- or 16-bit floating point numbers where the device supports them. Ordinary images store each channel as one of 256 steps, which would wreck slow processes: a trail fading by 3% per frame would get stuck at a dim floor, because 97% of a small step rounds back to the same step.

15.4Cellular automata: the Game of Life

A buffer can also be a grid of data. Here each texel is one cell of John Conway's Game of Life (1970), a "zero-player game" with two rules about each cell's eight neighbors:

  • A live cell with two or three live neighbors survives. Fewer and it dies of loneliness; more and it dies of overcrowding.
  • A dead cell with exactly three live neighbors comes alive.

That's all, yet from random soup emerge stable blocks, blinking oscillators and little "gliders" that walk across the grid forever. On a CPU you'd loop over every cell; here every cell updates at the same moment, which is exactly how the rules are defined. The buffer runs at a quarter of the screen's resolution so each cell is a visible block, with nearest filtering so cells stay crisp:

Two details: comparisons like n > 1.5 && n < 3.5 mean "2 or 3" while staying safe from tiny float errors, and the green channel is a second piece of memory per cell, a value that jumps to 1 when the cell lives and fades otherwise. A texel has four channels; simulations often use all of them as separate variables.

15.5Reaction–diffusion: how a leopard gets its spots

In 1952, Alan Turing (yes, that Turing) proposed that patterns like spots and stripes on animals could come from two chemicals that react with each other and diffuse (spread out) at different speeds. Here's one such system, the Gray–Scott model. Each pixel holds amounts of two chemicals, A (red channel) and B (green channel):

  • Reaction: B eats A to make more B. Where there's A and some B, B grows: A·B·B.
  • Feed and kill: A is constantly topped up (feed), and B constantly decays (kill).
  • Diffusion: both spread to their neighbors, A twice as fast as B.
Math from zero: diffusion and the Laplacian

Spreading means each cell moves toward the average of its neighbors. Pour ink in water: a spot with more ink than its surroundings loses some; a spot with less gains some. So the change in one step is proportional to (average of neighbors) − (me). That quantity is called the Laplacian.

The code computes it with weights: 0.2 for each of the four direct neighbors and 0.05 for each diagonal one. They add up to 1, so the weighted sum is a neighbor average, and subtracting the cell's own value gives "how far below its surroundings it is". Positive Laplacian: the cell is lower than its neighbors and will rise. Negative: it's a peak and will flatten.

The specks grow, split and settle into a maze of worm-like stripes and spots. Nothing in the code describes a stripe: the pattern emerges from local rules, the same way it does on a zebra. Different feed and kill values give completely different worlds. These defaults are what Karl Sims calls "coral growth"; try his "mitosis" setting, feed 0.0367 and kill 0.0649, for spots that keep dividing like cells.

15.6Flow: dye in a current

To make dye flow, you'd naturally think: each bit of dye moves along the current to its next position. But a pixel can only write its own color, not push color to another pixel. So turn the question around, and ask: where did my color come from? Look a little way upstream, against the current, and copy the color from there. This backwards trick is called semi-Lagrangian advection, and it's how real-time fluid simulations move things around.

The current itself comes from noise, but used cleverly. Chapter 11's noise is a landscape of hills and valleys. Its slope (gradient) points uphill, so following it would make dye pile up on the peaks. Turn the slope a quarter turn (Chapter 7's perpendicular) and you get a current that flows along the contour lines instead, circling every hill. Water flowing like that never piles up or thins out, much like a real liquid. This is called curl noise:

Give it a minute: the dye stretches into fine filaments that fold around invisible whirlpools. The vec2(0.0, iTime * 0.05) slowly slides the noise, so the whirlpools themselves drift over time. A full fluid simulation also computes how the current pushes on itself, which takes a few more passes, but the backwards look-up at its heart is exactly this line.

What you now know

  • A texture is a grid of texels. texture(s, uv) reads with normalized coordinates and filtering; texelFetch(s, ivec2, 0) reads one exact texel.
  • A pass can draw into a texture (a framebuffer). Reading it back next frame gives memory; two textures take turns (ping-pong).
  • In this course, Buffer A reads its own last frame on iChannel0, and Image reads Buffer A.
  • Initialize on iFrame == 0. Fading by a fraction per frame is exponential decay.
  • Feedback: read the past at transformed coordinates.
  • Cellular automata and reaction-diffusion: simple local rules over neighbors, every cell at once. The Laplacian is "neighbor average minus me".
  • Advection: each pixel copies the color from upstream. Curl noise flows around hills instead of up them.
