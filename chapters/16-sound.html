Music-reactive visuals

To make visuals dance, a shader needs to hear. It can't, directly: it can only read numbers. So this chapter starts from what sound physically is, follows it through a microphone and a famous algorithm into a small texture, and then wires that texture into everything you've built.

16.1What sound is

Sound is air pressure wobbling. A speaker cone pushes forward, squeezing the air in front of it, then pulls back, thinning it, hundreds or thousands of times a second. That wobble travels to your ear, and your eardrum wobbles along.

  • How fast it wobbles is the frequency, counted in wobbles per second, called hertz (Hz). It's what you hear as pitch. Humans hear roughly 20 Hz (a deep rumble) to 20,000 Hz (a faint whine). The A above middle C is 440 Hz.
  • How strongly it wobbles is the amplitude, which you hear as loudness.

A microphone turns the pressure into a voltage, and a computer measures that voltage at regular intervals, many thousands of times a second. Each measurement is a sample, a number between −1 and 1. CD audio takes 44,100 samples per second. So to a computer, a sound is just a very long list of numbers, which is good news for a shader.

Math from zero: why 44,100?

To capture a wobble, you need at least two samples per cycle, one near the top and one near the bottom; with fewer, a fast wave looks like a slower one (the same effect that makes wagon wheels seem to spin backwards in films). So a sampling rate captures frequencies up to half of itself, called the Nyquist frequency. 44,100 samples per second captures up to 22,050 Hz, just above the limit of human hearing. That's the reason for that particular number.

16.2Two views of a sound: waveform and spectrum

A list of samples over time is called the waveform. It's what you see in an audio editor: the actual wobble. But for visuals, a different view is usually more useful. In the early 1800s Joseph Fourier showed that any repeating wave can be built by adding up sine waves of different frequencies, each with its own amplitude. The list of "how much of each frequency" is the spectrum. A bass drum is mostly low frequencies; a cymbal is mostly high ones:

The waveform is the sum, and it looks complicated. The spectrum is the recipe, and it's simple: three bars. Real sounds have recipes with thousands of ingredients, changing many times a second, but the idea is the same.

From waveform to spectrum: the Fourier transform

Given only the waveform, how do you find the recipe? Here's the key idea, and it's an old friend. To measure how much of, say, a 200 Hz sine wave is in a sound, multiply the sound sample by sample with a 200 Hz test wave, and add up all the products. If the sound contains that frequency, the two rise and fall together, the products are mostly positive, and the sum is large. Any other frequency drifts in and out of step with the test wave, so its products are positive half the time and negative the other half, and they cancel out to almost nothing.

Multiply matching entries and add them up: that's a dot product (Chapter 7). And just as in Chapter 7, it measures how much two things "point the same way", here a sound and a test wave. Doing this for every frequency is the Fourier transform. (In practice each frequency is tested with both a sine and a cosine and the results combined, so it doesn't matter where in its cycle the sound's wave started.) The FFT, the Fast Fourier Transform, is a clever algorithm, published by Cooley and Tukey in 1965, that computes every frequency at once by reusing shared work. It's one of the most important algorithms ever written: it's in every phone, music app and video codec.

// AHA

A spectrum is a dot product between the sound and every possible sine wave. The same operation that lit a sphere in Chapter 7 tells you how much bass is in a song.

16.3Sound as a texture

The browser runs an FFT for you, through the Web Audio API's analyser. Every frame it takes the most recent 1,024 samples (about 23 milliseconds of sound) and turns them into 512 frequency bins. Each bin covers a slice of about 43 Hz: bin 0 is 0–43 Hz, bin 1 is 43–86 Hz, and so on up to about 22,000 Hz. Two adjustments make the numbers friendlier:

  • Loudness is reported in decibels, a logarithmic scale, because our ears work that way: ten times the sound power sounds only about twice as loud. The analyser maps −100 dB (very quiet) to 0 and −30 dB (very loud) to 1.
  • Each frame is blended with the previous ones, so the values don't flicker.

Then, following Shadertoy's convention, the numbers are packed into a texture 512 texels wide and 2 tall. Row 0 is the spectrum and row 1 is the waveform, so you read them at y = 0.25 and y = 0.75 (the centers of the two rows, Chapter 15):

float level = texture(iChannel0, vec2(x, 0.25)).r;   // loudness of frequency x: 0..1 (x = 0 is the bass end)
float wave  = texture(iChannel0, vec2(x, 0.75)).r;   // the waveform at position x: 0.5 means silence

Play some music and notice where the action is: almost everything happens in the left third. Music's energy is concentrated in the low and middle frequencies, and pitch works on a doubling scale (each octave up doubles the frequency), so all the bass notes of a piano squeeze into the first few bins while the top octave alone spans hundreds. To give the low end more room, visualizers often stretch it, for example reading at x * x instead of x.

16.4Bass, mids and treble

For visuals you rarely want 512 separate numbers. You want a few that mean something musical: how much bass (the kick drum and bass line), how much midrange (voices, most instruments), how much treble (cymbals, hi-hats, the "s" sounds). Average a range of bins for each:

Notice the pow(bass, 3.0). Raw loudness values are mushy: there's always some bass, so the disc barely changes size. Cubing pushes the in-between values toward zero while 1 stays 1, so the disc snaps on the beat and rests in between. Shaping the signal with Chapter 4's functions is most of the art of audio-reactive visuals. Try smoothstep(0.4, 0.8, bass) for a harder, more switch-like response.

16.7Sound with memory: a spectrogram

The audio texture is only "now": the last 23 milliseconds. Combine it with Chapter 15's buffers and you can keep a history. A spectrogram stacks the spectrum over time: each frame, shift the old picture one pixel to the left and paint the newest spectrum into the rightmost column. Time runs left to right, frequency bottom to top, loudness as brightness.

One wiring detail: in a Buffer pass, iChannel0 is already taken by the buffer's own last frame, so the sound arrives on iChannel1. (On Shadertoy you'd set that up yourself; Chapter 20 shows how.)

Play the beat and you can read the music: the kick drum makes regular bright blobs at the bottom, the hi-hats make thin streaks near the top, and the bass line and chords make horizontal bands at their pitches. Speech in the microphone shows the stacked lines (harmonics) of your voice moving up and down as you talk. Spectrograms are how audio engineers, birdsong researchers and speech scientists look at sound.

What you now know

  • Sound is a pressure wobble. Frequency is pitch (Hz); amplitude is loudness. Computers sample it, typically 44,100 times a second.
  • Any sound is a sum of sine waves. The waveform is the sum; the spectrum is the recipe.
  • The Fourier transform is a dot product with test waves at every frequency; the FFT computes it fast.
  • The audio texture is 512 × 2: spectrum at y = 0.25 (0 = bass end, values in decibels mapped to 0..1), waveform at y = 0.75 (0.5 = silence).
  • Average bins into bands, then shape them (pow, smoothstep) so motion snaps to the beat.
  • A buffer gives sound a memory. In a buffer pass the sound is on iChannel1.
