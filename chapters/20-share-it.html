Share it, and where to go next

20.1Shadertoy

Shadertoy is where shader people publish, comment and learn from each other's code, and every example in this course follows its conventions, so your code runs there unchanged. To publish:

  1. Make a free account and open shadertoy.com/new.
  2. In the workbench here, choose Export → Copy for Shadertoy, then select everything in Shadertoy's editor and paste over it. Press the play button under the editor (or Alt + Enter) to compile.
  3. Give it a name, tags (like 2d, sdf, noise, raymarching) and a one-line description, then save it as public or unlisted.

Pieces with a buffer (Chapter 15) need their channels wired up by hand, because Shadertoy lets each pass read from anything. The copied text includes these instructions as comments:

  • Click the + tab next to "Image" and choose Buffer A. Paste the Buffer A code there.
  • Below Buffer A's editor, click iChannel0 and choose Buffer A itself, so it reads its own last frame.
  • In the Image tab, set iChannel0 to Buffer A.
  • The small gear on a channel sets filtering (nearest or linear) and wrapping. Shadertoy buffers always run at full resolution, so the Game of Life's cells will be single pixels there unless you scale fragCoord yourself.

For sound (Chapter 16), click a channel and pick a track from the Music tab, the microphone or SoundCloud. The texture has the same 512 × 2 layout as here. In a pass with a buffer, put the sound on iChannel1 to match this course's code.

Shadertoy is also the world's biggest shader library. Open any shader, read its code with this course's eyes, change numbers and recompile to see what happens: your edits stay on your screen unless you save them. Reading other people's code is how most shader programmers learned.

20.2Into p5.js

Export → Copy as p5.js sketch wraps a single-pass shader into a complete sketch for the p5.js web editor. Paste it in and press play. Here's what the generated sketch does, and why, so you can adapt it:

  • It uses createFilterShader() in WEBGL mode. A filter shader runs over the whole canvas after you've drawn, like Photoshop's filters, and receives what p5 drew as a texture called tex0. The sketch renames it to iChannel0.
  • p5 provides vTexCoord (0..1 across the canvas) and canvasSize instead of fragCoord and iResolution, so the sketch computes them: fragCoord = texture coordinate × canvas size.
  • p5's coordinates run top to bottom, Shadertoy's bottom to top, so the sketch flips y in both fragCoord and texture reads (otherwise everything is upside down).
  • p5 finds the shader's entry point by looking for "main", and gets confused by a function called mainImage, so the sketch renames it.
  • Every frame, draw() sets the uniforms with setUniform: time from millis(), the mouse with y flipped, and so on. That's Chapter 18's frame loop, in p5's words.

The interesting part is the filter. Anything you draw in p5 before filter(fx) reaches the shader, which means a shader can be a lens over your old sketches: particles, flocking, text. 20.3Genuary

Genuary is a yearly event where thousands of people make generative art every day in January, one prompt per day, and post it with the #genuary tag. Prompts are things like "a grid", "one color", "make something that loops", "use noise". Every one of them can be answered with what you know now.

You don't need to do all 31. Pick the prompts that excite you, start each from your toolkit (below), and post them. The prompts appear on genuary.art in time for January, and every past year's prompts are archived there too, so you can warm up anytime with a past year's list.

20.4Where the shader people are

  • Shadertoy: comments under shaders are often generous with tips.
  • Social media: tags like #shader, #glsl, #creativecoding and #generativeart on Mastodon, Bluesky, Instagram and X, plus #genuary in January.
  • twigl (twigl.app): a playground for tiny shaders that fit in a social media post, home of the #つぶやきGLSL ("tweet GLSL") code-golf tradition.
  • The demoscene: a decades-old community making real-time art with code. Demoparties like Revision host live "shader showdowns", where coders battle on stage, writing shaders from scratch in 25 minutes.
  • Video: Inigo Quilez, The Art of Code (Martijn Steinrucken) and kishimisu all build shaders live on YouTube, explaining as they go. You'll follow nearly everything now.

20.6Where to go next

You've covered the ground most shader programmers stand on. Depending on what pulled you in, here are directions to go deeper:

  • Deeper 3D: Inigo Quilez's articles go much further on distance functions, lighting and terrain. After that, physically based rendering and path tracing (LearnOpenGL covers the lighting side).
  • Generative art: The Book of Shaders, read cover to cover now, will feel like review with new tricks. Then study artists' processes, for example Tyler Hobbs' essays on generative art.
  • Simulations: real fluid simulation (the GPU Gems chapter from Chapter 15), particles stored in textures, and eventually compute shaders.
  • WebGPU: the successor to WebGL, now available in major browsers. Its shading language, WGSL, looks different but works on the same ideas, and it adds compute shaders, which can do general work beyond coloring pixels.
  • Live visuals: Hydra for live-coded visuals in the browser, and tools like KodeLife or TouchDesigner for performing with shaders on stage.

What you now know

  • Shadertoy runs this course's code unchanged; buffers and sound need their channels wired by hand.
  • The p5.js export runs your shader as a filter: tex0 is the canvas, coordinates are flipped, mainImage is renamed, uniforms are set each frame.
  • A filter is a shader that reads a picture at bent coordinates.
  • Genuary, Shadertoy, twigl and the demoscene are where shader people share.
  • Your toolkit holds the course's most useful helpers; start every piece from it.
