Every pixel's only input is its position, so the first job in almost every shader is turning that position into numbers that are pleasant to do math with. This chapter builds the coordinate system you'll use for the rest of the course, starting from the number line, and ends with one line of code you'll write at the top of nearly every shader.

Coordinates from zero

Start with a number line: a straight line with 0 in the middle, positive numbers to the right and negative numbers to the left. Any position on the line is one number.

Now take a second number line, stand it upright and cross the first one at 0. Together they make a plane, and any point on the plane can be described by two numbers: how far right (x) and how far up (y). We write that pair as (x, y). The crossing point, (0, 0), is the origin. The horizontal line is the x axis, the vertical one the y axis.

  • (2, 1) is 2 to the right and 1 up.
  • (-1, 0.5) is 1 to the left and half up. Negative x means left of the origin; negative y means below it.
  • The two lines split the plane into four quadrants, according to the signs of x and y.

That's all a coordinate system is: an agreement about where (0, 0) is, which directions count as positive, and how big one unit is. The rest of this chapter is about choosing a good agreement for shaders.

fragCoord: counting pixels

The runner hands each pixel its position in fragCoord, measured in pixels. Its agreement is:

  • The origin (0, 0) is the bottom-left corner of the canvas.
  • x grows to the right, y grows upward. (In p5.js y grows downward, so this is flipped compared with what you know.)
  • One unit is one pixel, and fragCoord points at the center of the pixel, so the first pixel is at (0.5, 0.5), the next one to its right at (1.5, 0.5), and so on.

You don't have to take this on faith: hover the canvas in the workbench, and the bar along its bottom shows the exact fragCoord of the pixel under your mouse, along with that pixel's color.

On a high-density screen the canvas has more real pixels than its size on the page, often two for every page pixel in each direction. fragCoord counts the real ones. The inline previews in the text run at a lower resolution than the workbench, so the same shader sees different fragCoord ranges in each. This is exactly why you shouldn't rely on raw pixel numbers.

iResolution: how big is the canvas?

To make sense of a position you need to know the size of the canvas. The runner provides it as the uniform iResolution: iResolution.x is the width in pixels and iResolution.y is the height. (It's a vec3; the third number is the pixel's own aspect ratio, which is always 1.0, so you can ignore it. That's why you'll usually see iResolution.xy, a swizzle that takes the first two.)

Remember from Chapter 1 that a uniform is a value that is the same for every pixel in a frame. Every pixel sees the same iResolution, but its own fragCoord. The canvas can be any size (a phone, a laptop, a full-screen window), and iResolution is how the shader adapts.

Moving and stretching a range

A range from 0 to 1 is convenient for colors. For drawing shapes it's often nicer to have 0 in the middle, running from -1 to 1. Converting one range to another is called remapping, and it only needs two moves you already know from Chapter 2: multiplying stretches a range, and adding slides it.

Start with t from 0 to 1.

  • Multiply by 2: now it runs from 0 to 2. The range got twice as long.
  • Subtract 1: now it runs from -1 to 1. The whole range slid down by one.

So t * 2.0 - 1.0 turns 0..1 into -1..1. In general, to turn 0..1 into a range from low to high, stretch it by the new length and slide it to the new start:

new = t * (high - low) + low

Going backwards, from -1..1 to 0..1, undo the moves in reverse: add 1 (0..2), then divide by 2 (0..1). That's v * 0.5 + 0.5, a line you'll see constantly.

The squashed-circle problem

Something is wrong with these coordinates, and you'll see it as soon as you draw a circle. A circle is all the points within some distance of a center. GLSL has a function that measures a point's distance from (0, 0): length(p). (Chapter 7 builds it from Pythagoras.) So “inside a circle of radius 0.5” is length(p) < 0.5:

It's an oval, not a circle. The canvas is wider than it is tall, but x and y both run from -1 to 1, so one unit of x covers more pixels than one unit of y. Space is stretched sideways, and everything drawn in it is stretched too.

The fix is to measure both directions with the same ruler. Divide both x and y by the same number, the height, instead of by their own sizes. Then one unit means the same number of pixels in both directions. y still runs across the full height, while x runs a bit further, because the canvas is wider. For a 16:9 canvas x reaches 16/9 ≈ 1.78.

Centered coordinates: the line you'll always write

Putting it all together, step by step:

  1. Move the origin to the center. Subtract half the canvas size: fragCoord - 0.5 * iResolution.xy. Now (0, 0) is the middle, and x runs from minus half the width to plus half the width, in pixels.
  2. Use one ruler. Divide both by the height: / iResolution.y. Now y runs from -0.5 to 0.5, and x the same amount per pixel.
  3. Make y span -1 to 1. Multiply by 2.

Multiply out the 2 and the three steps collapse into one line:

vec2 uv = (fragCoord * 2.0 - iResolution.xy) / iResolution.y;

This is the line that starts most shaders on Shadertoy and in this course. (Check the algebra: 2 × (fragCoord - 0.5 × iResolution) = 2 × fragCoord - iResolution.) With it, the circle is round:

You choose your coordinate system. One line turns raw pixel counts into a clean mathematical space with the origin in the middle and equal units in both directions, and from then on you can forget about pixels.

Zooming and panning

Once you have centered coordinates, two more moves change your view of the space:

  • Multiplying zooms out. uv *= 2.0 makes every pixel's coordinates twice as big, so the canvas now spans twice as much space. Everything you draw looks half the size.
  • Adding pans. uv += vec2(0.5, 0.0) shifts every pixel's coordinates right by 0.5, so the drawing slides left.

Both feel backwards at first: multiplying by 2 makes things smaller, adding to x moves things left. That's because you're changing the coordinates you look at the world with, not the world. Chapter 9 makes this idea precise; it's one of the most important in the course.

Load the coordinate viewer into the workbench and add uv *= 2.0; right after the first line. Predict how many grid squares you'll see, then try uv += vec2(0.5, 0.0); and predict which way the circle moves.

What happens

With uv *= 2.0 the circle shrinks to half its size and you see twice as many grid lines in each direction. With uv += vec2(0.5, 0.0) the circle slides to the left: the pixel in the middle of the canvas now has x = 0.5, so the circle's center (x = 0) appears where the pixels have x = -0.5.

The mouse: iMouse

The runner also tells every pixel where the mouse is, in iMouse, a vec4. While you drag on the canvas, iMouse.xy is the mouse position in pixels, in the same system as fragCoord: origin at the bottom-left. iMouse.z is positive while the button is held down. Before your first click it's all zeros.

What you now know

  • A coordinate system is an agreement: where the origin is, which ways are positive, and how big a unit is.
  • fragCoord is in pixels, with the origin at the bottom-left and y pointing up; pixel centers sit at .5.
  • iResolution.xy is the canvas size. fragCoord / iResolution.xy runs 0..1 in both directions.
  • Multiply to stretch a range, add to slide it: t * 2.0 - 1.0 maps 0..1 to -1..1, and v * 0.5 + 0.5 maps it back.
  • Dividing x and y by different sizes squashes shapes. Divide both by the height instead.
  • The standard line: vec2 uv = (fragCoord * 2.0 - iResolution.xy) / iResolution.y;
  • Multiplying coordinates zooms out; adding pans the other way. iMouse.xy converts with the same formula.
