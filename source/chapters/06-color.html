You know a color is three numbers. This chapter is about choosing good ones: how light mixes, why green looks brighter than blue at the same value, how to build gradients and whole palettes from a formula, and two technical ideas, gamma and tone mapping, that separate muddy pictures from glowing ones.

Light, not paint

A screen makes color by adding light, so its colors mix the way spotlights do, not the way paints do. Paint absorbs light, so mixing paints removes more and gets darker. Light adds, so mixing lights gets brighter. This is called additive color. Three overlapping spotlights show every combination at once:

Red and green overlap into yellow, green and blue into cyan, red and blue into magenta, and all three into white. Each col += line adds one light. You'll write col += for glows and light effects throughout the course, precisely because light adds.

The space of all colors

Since a color is three numbers between 0 and 1, every color is a point inside a cube: red along one edge, green along another, blue along the third. Black is one corner (0, 0, 0), white the opposite corner (1, 1, 1), and the line between them is all the greys. Brightness and grey

Equal amounts of red, green and blue always make a grey, from black to white. But our eyes don't treat the three channels equally. We're far more sensitive to green than to blue, so pure green at full value looks much brighter than pure blue at full value. The perceived brightness, called luminance, is a weighted sum:

luminance ≈ 0.299 × red + 0.587 × green + 0.114 × blue

In GLSL that's dot(col, vec3(0.299, 0.587, 0.114)) (the dot function multiplies matching slots and adds them; Chapter 7 explains it). It's the right way to turn a color into grey:

Each of the three has one channel at full strength, yet the greys underneath are very different. This matters whenever you pick colors: a palette with pure blue next to pure green will look lopsided.

Multiplying and adding colors

Two operations do most of the work in color code, and each has a physical meaning:

  • Multiplying two colors is filtering: the light passes through something that lets each channel through by a certain amount (Chapter 2's blue glass). Multiplying by a single number dims or brightens: col * 0.5 is half as bright. Multiplying is also how lighting works in 3D: the light's color times the surface's color.
  • Adding is more light. Adding a glow on top of a scene, adding the light from several sources.

Addition has one danger: values can pass 1.0, and the screen clamps them to white. The section on tone mapping below deals with that.

Hue as an angle: HSV

RGB is how screens think, but not how people describe color. People think in hue (which color: red, orange, yellow…), saturation (how vivid versus greyish) and value (how bright). That's the HSV model. The hues form a circle, the color wheel, so hue is naturally an angle, written here as a number from 0 to 1 for one full turn.

How does that one-line conversion work? Split the hue circle into six equal parts. Each channel follows the same flat-topped shape around the circle: fully on for a third of the way, ramping down over a sixth, off for a third, ramping back up over a sixth. The three channels use the same shape shifted around the circle by a third of a turn each (the 0, 4 and 2 are in sixths). mod(… , 6.0) wraps the circle, abs(… - 3.0) - 1.0 builds the flat-topped ramp, and clamp cuts it off at 0 and 1. Then saturation mixes toward white (no hue), and value scales everything.

Notice the bottom edge is white, not grey. Saturation 0 means "no hue at all", which leaves a grey exactly as bright as the value, and with value 1.0 that grey is white. Change val to 0.5 and the bottom becomes mid-grey.

The big win: animating hue is now one number. hsv2rgb(vec3(iTime * 0.1, 0.8, 1.0)) cycles smoothly around the rainbow.

Cosine palettes

Inigo Quilez, one of the creators of Shadertoy, found a way to describe an entire palette with four vec3 numbers. It uses what you learned in Chapter 5: give each channel its own cosine wave.

color(t) = a + b * cos(6.28318 * (c * t + d))
  • a: the middle brightness each wave swings around.
  • b: how far each wave swings (its amplitude).
  • c: how many times each wave cycles as t goes from 0 to 1 (its frequency).
  • d: how far each wave is shifted (its phase). Different shifts per channel are what create hues.

The bottom half of this canvas plots the three waves and the top half shows the colors they make together:

Change d to vec3(0.0, 0.1, 0.2). What will the three waves do, and what happens to the colors?

The three waves bunch together, so red, green and blue rise and fall almost in unison. When the channels are nearly equal, the color is nearly grey, so the palette becomes a dark-to-light ramp with a warm tint. Spread the shifts apart again and the hues come back. Hue is literally the offset between three waves.

A palette is a function from a number to a color, so you can feed it anything: a distance, a time, a position. Here it colors rings by their distance from the center, sliding with time:

A palette is three waves out of step. Four numbers in, endless coherent color out, and anything that produces a number (a distance, a time, a tile number) can be turned into color with it.

Why 0.5 isn't half as bright: gamma

Here's a surprise: a pixel with value 0.5 does not give off half as much light as a pixel with value 1.0. It gives off about a fifth. Screens apply a curve called gamma: the light that comes out is roughly the value raised to the power 2.2.

The reason is historical and practical. Our eyes are much more sensitive to differences between dark shades than bright ones, so storing colors with this curve spends more of the available numbers on the darks, where we notice. Image files and screens agree on this curve (it's part of the standard called sRGB).

For picking colors by eye, it doesn't matter: you see what you get. It matters when you do light math, like mixing light or computing lighting in 3D, because physical light adds linearly. The rule: do light math on linear values, and convert to screen values at the very end with pow(col, vec3(1.0 / 2.2)). You'll see that line at the end of every 3D shader in Chapters 13 and 14.

The top gradient sags into a dark olive in the middle, because (0.5, 0.5, 0) on screen is much less light than either end. The bottom one, mixed as real light, stays bright and passes through a clear yellow, which is what you'd see mixing two real lights.

Too bright: tone mapping

When you add lots of light, values go past 1.0 and get clamped. Everything above 1 becomes the same flat white, and detail is lost. Tone mapping squeezes any brightness from 0 to infinity into 0..1 smoothly instead of chopping it. A simple, good curve is:

col = 1.0 - exp(-col) // 0 stays 0, 1 becomes 0.63, 3 becomes 0.95, never reaches 1

On the left, the middle of the glow is a flat blob where the red channel is clipped and the color shifts toward yellow-white abruptly. On the right, the glow rolls off smoothly. The right side is also a little dimmer overall, because the curve bends every value down a bit. Photographers call the fix exposure: multiply before the curve, as in 1.0 - exp(-light * 2.0), and try a few values. This line is cheap insurance in any shader that adds light.

What you now know

  • Screens add light: red + green = yellow, all three = white. Use col += for light.
  • Every color is a point in the RGB cube; equal channels are greys.
  • Green looks brightest, blue darkest: luminance ≈ dot(col, vec3(0.299, 0.587, 0.114)).
  • Chain mix with smoothstep for multi-stop gradients. Multiply to filter or dim, add for more light.
  • HSV describes color as hue (an angle), saturation and value.
  • Cosine palettes: a + b * cos(6.28318 * (c * t + d)), four vec3s for a whole palette.
  • Screen values follow a gamma curve: do light math in linear values, then pow(col, vec3(1.0 / 2.2)). Tone map bright light with 1.0 - exp(-col).
