A shader can't copy a shape: there's no shape to copy, only pixels asking questions. But it can bend space so that many different pixels end up asking the same question. That's how one circle becomes a thousand, one tooth becomes a gear, and one wedge becomes a kaleidoscope, without a single loop.

Tiling: fract makes copies

Recall fract(x) from Chapter 4: it keeps only the part after the decimal point, so it climbs from 0 to 1 and jumps back, over and over. To let shapes overlap tile borders you'd have to check the neighboring tiles too, which is exactly the trick Voronoi uses in Chapter 11.

Which tile am I in? floor

floor(x) is fract's partner: it keeps only the whole-number part, so floor(p) is the same for every pixel in a tile and different for each tile. It's the tile's id, its address in the grid, like (2, −1). Every pixel can split its position into "which tile" and "where in the tile":

p = floor(p) + fract(p) which tile where inside it

Polar coordinates, from zero

So far, every position has been "how far right, how far up": Cartesian coordinates, after the philosopher Descartes. There's another way to say where a point is: "how far from the center, and in which direction". That's polar coordinates: a radius r and an angle a.

Polar to Cartesian is the unit circle from Chapter 5, scaled: the point at angle a on a circle of radius r is

p = r * vec2(cos(a), sin(a))

Cartesian to polar goes the other way. The radius is the distance from the center, length(p). The angle needs the inverse of cos and sin: given a point, which angle points at it? That's the arctangent. GLSL's two-argument version, atan(p.y, p.x) (note: y first), returns the angle from the positive x axis, counterclockwise, in radians from −π to π.

r = length(p) a = atan(p.y, p.x) // -π (pointing left, from below) .. π (pointing left, from above)

Why −π to π and not 0 to 2π? Both describe a full turn. The jump from π back to −π happens on the negative x axis, the line pointing left from the center. Remember that line; it's called the seam, and it causes one classic bug, coming up shortly.

Polar coordinates are just another grid, bent around a point. Draw ordinary grid lines in (angle, radius) and you get a spiderweb: lines of equal radius are rings, and lines of equal angle are rays:

Flowers, stars and spirals

In polar coordinates a circle is simply r < radius. Let the radius depend on the angle, and the circle's edge wobbles: five bumps around the circle make five petals:

Set petals to 5.5. What goes wrong, and where?

A crack appears on the left side, along the seam. As the angle crosses the seam it jumps from π to −π, a jump of a full turn. With 5 petals, cos(a * 5.0) jumps by 5 full turns, which cos doesn't notice. With 5.5 it jumps by 5.5 turns, and half a turn of cos is a flip from +1 to −1. The rule: anything you do with the angle must repeat a whole number of times around the circle.

Kaleidoscopes

A real kaleidoscope is a tube with mirrors along its length: you see one small wedge of colored glass, reflected over and over. An infinite tunnel

One more polar trick, and it's a famous one from the 1990s demoscene. Use the angle and one over the radius as texture coordinates, and the flat screen turns into a tunnel you fly through:

Imagine standing inside a long pipe of radius R, looking straight down it. A ring of the pipe's wall at distance z in front of you appears on screen as a circle. How big? Perspective makes things look smaller in proportion to their distance: twice as far, half the size. So the ring appears with screen radius r = R / z.

Turn that around: z = R / r. A pixel at screen radius r is looking at the bit of wall at depth R / r. That's the 0.25 / r in the code (R = 0.25). Near the center, r is tiny and the depth is huge: the far end of the tunnel. The angle tells you where around the pipe you're looking. So (angle, depth) is the position on the pipe's wall, and a pattern drawn in those coordinates lands on the wall in perfect perspective. A 3D effect, without any 3D.

What you now know

  • fract(p * n) - 0.5 gives every tile its own centered coordinates; one shape, infinite copies. Shapes can't cross into neighboring tiles.
  • floor(p * n) is the tile's id: use it for checkerboards (mod(id.x + id.y, 2.0)), offsets and per-tile variation.
  • abs is a mirror; abs(p) mirrors four ways.
  • Polar: r = length(p), a = atan(p.y, p.x) (−π..π), and back with r * vec2(cos(a), sin(a)). Repeat a whole number of times around, or the seam shows.
  • Flowers wobble the radius with the angle; spirals mix angle and radius in one stripe.
  • Angular repetition: mod the angle into one slice; add abs for mirrored slices, a kaleidoscope.
  • Fold, shift, rotate, repeat multiplies complexity. (angle, 1/r) is a tunnel.
