There are no triangles, meshes or 3D engines in this chapter. A whole 3D scene will be one distance function, like the ones from Chapter 8 but with a z, plus a loop that walks a ray into the scene for every pixel. By the end, you'll have lit, shaded objects standing on a floor, and you'll know what every line does.

A ray for every pixel

A camera is a point, the eye, looking through a window: the screen. Every pixel is a tiny pane in that window. To find what a pixel sees, shoot a ray from the eye through the pixel's pane and out into the scene; whatever surface the ray hits first is what that pixel shows.

In numbers: put the eye at a point called ro (ray origin). Put the screen a distance f in front of it. The pixel with centered coordinates uv (Chapter 3) sits on the screen at (uv.x, uv.y, f) relative to the eye, so the ray's direction is that arrow, normalized:

f is the focal length, and it sets the field of view. A small f puts the screen close to the eye, so the rays fan out wide: a wide-angle lens. A big f narrows the fan: a zoom lens. (Since uv spans −1..1 vertically, the vertical field of view is 2·atan(1/f): 90° for f = 1, about 67° for f = 1.5.)

Walking the ray: sphere tracing

Now the key question: where along the ray is the first surface? For a single sphere you could solve an equation. For a scene made of dozens of distance functions combined with min and smin, you can't. But the distance function itself offers a promise you met in Chapter 8's probe circle: nothing is closer than this distance. So:

  1. Start at the eye, with t = 0.
  2. Ask map for the distance d at the current point.
  3. Nothing is within d in any direction, so it's safe to move d along the ray. Do it: t += d.
  4. Repeat. If d gets tiny, you're touching a surface: a hit. If t gets huge, the ray has escaped into the sky.

This is called sphere tracing or raymarching, because each step is the radius of a sphere that's guaranteed to be empty. In open space the steps are big; near a surface they shrink, so the ray never overshoots. In code it's a short loop:

One more view before moving on. This one colors each pixel by how many steps its ray took. It's a debugging view, and it shows something surprising:

Rays that hit a surface head-on finish in a few steps. Rays that skim past an edge take the most, because they stay close to a surface for a long time and every step is tiny. The sky near the horizon is expensive too: those rays run almost parallel to the floor. This is why raymarchers set a step limit, and it's what to look at when a scene runs slowly (Chapter 19). A ray that runs out of steps before reaching anything counts as a miss: that's what the few dark specks along the sphere's lower edge in the depth view are.

Which way does the surface face? Normals

Chapter 7 lit a ball with dot(normal, light), where the normal is the direction a surface faces. For a ball the normal was easy. For an arbitrary scene, the distance function knows it, and here's how to ask.

Stand at a point on a surface and take a tiny step in some direction. If you step straight away from the surface, the distance grows as fast as possible: by exactly the length of the step. Step along the surface, and the distance doesn't change. So "the direction in which the distance grows fastest" is the direction pointing straight out of the surface: the normal. Mathematicians call that direction the gradient.

To measure it, nudge the point a tiny amount along x, y and z separately and see how much the distance changes each time:

Each line measures the change in distance across a tiny step in one direction, the way you'd measure the slope of a hill by checking the height a step to the left and a step to the right. Together the three changes form an arrow pointing uphill in distance, which is out of the surface. This trick is called finite differences, and the swizzles e.xyy and friends (Chapter 2) are just a compact way to write the three tiny steps.

Light

Real lighting is complicated, but three simple ingredients get you most of the way. Each one is a dot product or close to it:

  • Diffuse (Chapter 7): a matte surface is as bright as it faces the light. max(dot(n, lightDir), 0.0), where lightDir is the direction toward the light.
  • Ambient: in real life, light bounces around and the sky glows, so surfaces facing away from the sun aren't pitch black. A cheap stand-in: a dim bluish light that's strongest on surfaces facing up, 0.5 + 0.5 * n.y.
  • Specular: shiny surfaces show a bright highlight where they reflect the light toward your eye. That happens where the normal points halfway between "toward the light" and "toward the eye". So compute that halfway direction h = normalize(lightDir - rd) (the direction toward the eye is -rd) and check how closely the normal matches it: pow(max(dot(n, h), 0.0), 64.0). The power controls how tight the highlight is: higher looks glossier.

Light math belongs in linear values, so the scene ends with Chapter 6's gamma correction. And to color objects differently, map returns two numbers: the distance and a material id saying which object is nearest:

Read mainImage top to bottom and you can name every step: a ray per pixel, march it, find the normal, pick the material, add up three kinds of light, convert to screen values. This structure is the skeleton of nearly every raymarched shader on Shadertoy.

The sphere and the box don't cast shadows on the floor. Why not, given that the light code is correct?

Diffuse lighting only asks "does this surface face the light?", never "is anything in the way?". The floor under the sphere faces straight up, so it's fully lit. Shadows need a second question per pixel: march a ray from the surface toward the light, and see if it hits anything. That's the first thing Chapter 14 adds.

A 3D renderer is a distance function, a loop and a few dot products. Rays march by safe distances, normals come from how the distance changes, and light is how much a surface faces the sun.

What you now know

  • 3D SDFs work like 2D ones: sphere length(p) - r, box, ground p.y - h; combine with min.
  • A camera is an eye ro and a ray per pixel rd = normalize(vec3(uv, f)); f sets the field of view.
  • Sphere tracing: step along the ray by the distance the SDF returns, until it's tiny (hit) or t is huge (miss).
  • Rays that graze surfaces take the most steps.
  • The normal is the direction in which distance grows fastest; finite differences measure it.
  • Lighting: diffuse dot(n, l), ambient 0.5 + 0.5 * n.y, specular with the halfway vector, then gamma.
  • map can return a material id alongside the distance.
