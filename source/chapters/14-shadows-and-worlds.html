Chapter 13's scene was correct but plain: no shadows, no air, a camera stuck looking straight ahead. Each of those is a few more lines on the same skeleton. This chapter adds them one at a time, then uses everything to build three worlds: a sculpture at golden hour, an infinite lattice, and a mountain range made of noise.

Shadows: is anything in the way?

Diffuse lighting asks whether a surface faces the light. A shadow asks whether anything blocks it. You already have the tool for that question: from the point you're shading, march a second ray toward the light. If it hits something, the light is blocked.

Why start at t = 0.02? The point you're shading is on a surface, so map there is almost zero, and a ray starting exactly there would immediately "hit" the very surface it started from, shadowing everything. Starting a hair away avoids that. (Graphics people call the speckled mess you get otherwise "shadow acne".)

Real shadows have soft edges, because the sun isn't a point: it's a disc in the sky, and near a shadow's edge only part of it is hidden. There's a beautiful trick to fake this almost for free. While marching toward the light, the ray passes things at various distances. At each step, h / t is roughly the angle by which the ray misses the nearest object, as seen from the shaded point. If that near-miss angle is small compared with the sun's size, the sun is partly covered. So keep the smallest value of k * h / t along the way:

Ambient occlusion: creases are darker

The ambient light from Chapter 13 is a stand-in for light arriving from the whole sky. But a point deep in a crease or a corner can only see a small patch of sky, so it should get less. That darkening is called ambient occlusion (AO).

The distance function can estimate it. From a surface point, step out a little way along the normal. In open space the distance there equals how far you stepped: nothing is near. In a crease, other surfaces are close, so the distance is less than the step. The shortfall measures how enclosed the point is:

Fog: the air between

Air isn't perfectly clear. Over a long distance it scatters light, so faraway things fade toward the color of the sky. Physically, every meter of air lets through the same fraction of light, say 99%. After two meters, 99% of 99%; after t meters, 0.99t. A quantity that shrinks by the same fraction per step is an exponential decay (Chapter 4), so:

visible = exp(-density * t) // how much of the object's light gets through col = mix(col, skyColor, 1.0 - visible) // the rest is replaced by the sky's color

Shaders often use exp(-density * t * t) instead, which keeps nearby things perfectly clear and then fades faster. Fog does three jobs at once: depth (far looks far), mood (the fog's color sets the time of day) and hiding the place where the scene ends.

A camera that can look anywhere

So far the camera has always looked straight down the z axis. To look at any point from anywhere, you need the camera's own three directions, expressed in world coordinates: forward, right and up. Chapter 7's cross product finds them:

This is Chapter 9's rotation idea, one dimension up. In Chapter 13 the ray recipe was "uv.x steps right, uv.y steps up, f steps forward" with right = (1, 0, 0), up = (0, 1, 0) and forward = (0, 0, 1). Now the same recipe uses the camera's turned arrows. The cross product of world-up and forward is perpendicular to both, which is a horizontal arrow pointing to the camera's right, so the horizon stays level. (Looking straight up or down breaks it, since forward and world-up are then parallel and their cross product is zero.)

Bending space, and its price

Chapter 9 transformed coordinates before asking the shape. You can do that with transforms that vary from place to place. Twisting rotates each horizontal slice by an angle that depends on its height. Displacement adds a wiggle to the distance itself:

Both tricks break the distance promise. After twisting, two points that were close together may have been rotated by different amounts, so the returned "distance" can be larger than the real one, and a full step might jump right through a surface. The fix is to step only a fraction of the distance (t += d * 0.6), which costs more steps but stays safe. Change 0.6 to 1.0 and watch for torn surfaces when the twist is strong.

Mountains from noise

A landscape is a heightfield: for every spot (x, z) on the ground, a function says how high the land is there. Chapter 11's fBm makes a great height function. A point is above the land when its y is bigger than the height below it, so p.y - terrain(p.xz) works as a (rough) distance:

Three details worth noticing:

  • Half steps. The height difference straight down isn't the true distance: on a steep slope the nearest rock might be off to the side and much closer. Stepping half the height keeps the ray safe.
  • Relative precision. h < 0.002 * t accepts a hit sooner the farther away it is. A faraway mountain is only a pixel or two on screen, so there's no point marching to perfect precision.
  • Materials from geometry. The normal's y says how flat the ground is (1 = flat), so flat areas get grass and steep ones rock. Height adds snow, but only where it's flat enough for snow to settle.

What you now know

  • Shadows: march from the surface toward the light; start a little off the surface.
  • Soft shadows: keep the smallest k * h / t, the closest near-miss relative to distance.
  • Ambient occlusion: probe along the normal; if the distance is less than the step, the point is enclosed.
  • Fog is exponential decay: mix(col, sky, 1.0 - exp(-density * t * t)).
  • A look-at camera builds forward, right and up with cross products, then follows the same ray recipe.
  • mod(p + c, 2c) - c repeats 3D space for free. Twists and displacements need smaller steps.
  • Terrain: p.y - height(p.xz), half steps, precision relative to distance, materials from the normal.
