A shader has no console.log, no breakpoints and no way to print a number. When something goes wrong, you get either a compile error or a picture that's wrong in some way. This chapter turns that around: the picture is your debugger, once you know how to make it show you what's going on.

Reading compile errors

When code doesn't compile, the GPU driver says why, in a terse format. In the workbench the red bar under the editor shows the message, highlights the line, and adds a plain-words explanation. A raw message looks like this:

ERROR: 0:12: 'radius' : undeclared identifier

Read it as: "error, in source 0, on line 12: the word radius: it hasn't been declared". Three parts: where, which word, what's wrong. Most errors you'll meet are one of a handful:

Message containsUsually means
undeclared identifierA typo, wrong capitalization (GLSL is case-sensitive), or using a variable before or outside the block where it's declared.
cannot convert from 'const int' to 'float'You wrote 1 where GLSL needs 1.0 (Chapter 2). The most common error of all.
no matching overloaded functionA built-in got the wrong kinds of values: mix(vec3, float, float), or length() of nothing.
syntax errorPunctuation: a missing ; or bracket. Often reported on the line after the real mistake, because that's where the compiler noticed.
reserved word, illegal use of reserved wordYou named a variable smooth, sample, input, output, filter or half.
redefinitionDeclaring the same name twice in one block: drop the type the second time.
l-value required, can't modifyAssigning to something read-only: a uniform like iTime, or an in parameter.
not enough data provided for constructionA constructor with the wrong number of values, like vec3(1.0, 2.0).

Make the invisible visible

When code compiles but the picture is wrong, something in the middle has a value you didn't expect. You can't print it, but you can draw it: put the value into the output color and look. Since every pixel draws its own value, you see the whole field of values at once, which is often more useful than one printed number would be.

The basic moves:

  • A float as grey: fragColor = vec4(vec3(v), 1.0); Only 0 to 1 is visible: everything below 0 looks black and everything above 1 looks white, so you can't tell 1.0 from 500.0.
  • Positive and negative: vec3(max(v, 0.0), 0.0, max(-v, 0.0)) shows positive values in red and negative ones in blue. Perfect for signed distances.
  • Directions: n * 0.5 + 0.5 maps −1..1 to 0..1, as in Chapter 13's normals view.
  • Contours: fract(v * 10.0) draws a band every 0.1, revealing the shape of a field and how fast it changes, as in Chapter 8's field view.
  • Freeze time: replace iTime with a constant, so you can study one moment.

Combining these into a reusable helper is worth it. This one shows 0..1 as grey with bands every 0.1, values below 0 in blue, values above 1 in orange, infinities in yellow and NaN in flashing magenta, so nothing hides:

Keep debugValue in your toolkit and drop it in anywhere: fragColor = vec4(debugValue(d), 1.0); return; shows you the value of d across the whole canvas. (Some drivers implement isnan loosely, assuming NaN never happens. If NaN slips past it, the pixels usually come out black, which is a clue in itself.)

Divide and conquer

When you don't know where the problem is, shrink the search:

  • Return early. Show an intermediate value and return; right after, halfway down mainImage. If it already looks wrong, the bug is above; if not, below. Repeat, halving each time.
  • Remove ingredients. Replace a noise call with a constant, a texture read with a plain color, a loop's count with 1. When the problem disappears, the last thing you removed is involved.
  • Simplify the inputs. Freeze time, put the camera in a simple spot, test with a sphere instead of your full scene.
  • Check your assumptions out loud. "This should be 1 at the center and 0 at the edge." Then draw it and see whether it is. Most bugs are a value with the wrong range, the wrong sign or the wrong units.

Hunting NaN and infinity

NaN (Chapter 17) is the most confusing kind of bug, because it spreads silently and looks different on different GPUs: black on yours, garbage on a friend's phone. The usual sources, and their fixes:

SourceFix
normalize(v) where v can be zeroMake sure it can't be, or use v / max(length(v), 1e-6).
sqrt(x) where x can dip below zerosqrt(max(x, 0.0)), as in Chapter 9's lit ball.
pow(x, y) with negative xpow(abs(x), y), or clamp x to be positive. (pow is undefined for x < 0.)
acos(d) or asin(d) of a dot productRounding can push a dot product of unit vectors to 1.0000001, outside acos's range. acos(clamp(d, -1.0, 1.0)).
x / y where y can be zero; log(0.0)These give infinity, which turns into NaN as soon as it meets 0.0 * inf or inf - inf. Add a tiny amount: x / max(y, 1e-6).

A field guide to common bugs

Symptoms you'll meet again and again, and where to look first:

  • The whole canvas is black. A NaN early on; a color that's always negative; a distance compared the wrong way round (d > 0.0 instead of d < 0.0); a forgotten fragColor =.
  • The whole canvas is white, or flat. Values far above 1 (glows adding up, missing tone map), or a coordinate that doesn't vary: dividing by the wrong thing, or using iResolution where you meant fragCoord.
  • Circles come out as ovals. x and y divided by different numbers (Chapter 3): divide both by iResolution.y.
  • Upside down. Something used top-down coordinates: image files, the p5.js bridge, or mouse code (Chapter 18).
  • A crack or seam along one line. The atan seam (Chapter 10), a fract or mod jump, or a tile border.
  • Jagged, shimmering or moiré edges. Aliasing: use smooth edges with a pixel-sized width, and fade out detail that's finer than a pixel (Chapter 8).
  • Banding in smooth, dark gradients. The screen's 256 steps per channel. Add a touch of grain (Chapter 12).
  • Raymarched surfaces with holes, speckles or missing parts. Too few steps, or a distance that overestimates: after scaling, twisting or displacing, step more carefully (Chapter 14).
  • Everything in shadow, or speckled shadows. The light direction must point toward the light; start shadow rays slightly off the surface (Chapter 14).
  • Fine at first, choppy after a while. iTime has grown large and lost precision (Chapter 17).
  • Fine on your computer, broken on a phone. Lower precision, a NaN that your GPU happened to hide, or plain slowness.

You can't print from a shader, but you can paint every value at once. Draw the number you're unsure about, with a view that shows its range and sign, and most bugs become obvious in seconds.

Three bugs to fix

Each example below has one bug. Load it into the workbench, use the techniques above to find it, and fix it. Each has a hint and the solution.

Bug 1: the dark ball

Hint

Show l * 0.5 + 0.5 as the color. Which way is l pointing: toward the upper left, or away from it? And which part of the ball is lit?

Solution

The arrow from A to B is B - A (Chapter 7). The code computes n - lightPos, the arrow from the light to the surface: exactly backwards. So the ball is lit on the lower right, the side facing away from the light. The fix is vec3 l = normalize(lightPos - n);.

Bug 2: everything in shadow

Hint

Show the shadow value alone as the color: it's 0 everywhere. Now think about where the shadow ray starts, and what map returns at that exact spot.

Solution

The shadow ray starts exactly on the surface it's shading, where map is (almost) zero, so the very first check "hits" the surface itself, everywhere. This is shadow acne at its most extreme (Chapter 14). Start a little way along the ray: float t = 0.02; (or start from p + n * 0.01).

Bug 3: washed out

Hint

Paste in debugValue and look at col.r, col.g and col.b one at a time. How much of the canvas is above 1?

Solution

Six glows of 0.06 / d add up to far more than 1 across most of the canvas, and the screen clips everything above 1 to the same white, so the colors are lost. Either make each glow weaker (0.01 / d), or keep the glow and tone map at the end (Chapter 6): col = 1.0 - exp(-col);. Tone mapping keeps the brightness and the colors.

When it's slow

Performance problems are bugs too. The workbench shows its own symptom: when frames take too long it lowers the resolution, so a shader that suddenly looks soft is a shader that's too slow. To find out why:

  • Make a heat map of the expensive loop, as in Chapter 13: count iterations and show the count as color. You'll see exactly where the time goes.
  • Remove parts and watch the frame rate: shadows, AO, octaves of noise, the number of steps.
  • Exit loops early and skip lighting for pixels that hit nothing.
  • Precompute what doesn't change each frame into a buffer (Chapter 15).

What you now know

  • Error messages: where (line), which word, what's wrong. Fix the first one first; syntax errors often show up a line late.
  • Draw what you can't print: grey, signed red/blue, directions, contours, frozen time.
  • A debugValue helper shows every range, including NaN and infinity, at a glance.
  • The inspector reads exact values in 0..1: scale and shift into range first.
  • Isolate by returning early, removing ingredients and simplifying inputs.
  • Guard NaN sources: normalize of zero, sqrt and pow of negatives, acos out of range, division by zero.
